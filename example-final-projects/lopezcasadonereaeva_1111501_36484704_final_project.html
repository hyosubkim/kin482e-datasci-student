Exploring the Role of Sex and Genetic Variants in Alzheimer’s Pathophysiology: Insights from Amyloid PET Imaging¶

KIN_V 482E Final Project by Nerea Eva Lopez and Maée Paroz¶

01.12.2024¶

Introduction¶

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder responsible for the majority of dementia cases worldwide [1]. While its biological mechanisms remain incompletely understood, research continues to uncover potential risk factors and biomarkers, such as specific genetic variants and amyloid accumulation [2].

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is marked by the presence of extracellular amyloid plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles. The prevailing model of AD pathogenesis suggests that the accumulation of Aβ (amyloid-beta) peptide triggers a cascade of downstream events, ultimately resulting in cognitive impairment [3].

Over 80 genetic regions have been linked to Alzheimer's disease. However, the condition is often influenced by a combination of multiple genes, along with lifestyle and environmental factors. Nevertheless, carrying multiple risk-associated genetic variants can increase the likelihood of developing Alzheimer's, highlighting the need to better understand the role of these genetic variants. [4]

AD is marked by pronounced sex differences in incidence, pathophysiology and progression [5]. Certain genetic variants showed differing effects between females and males, emphasizing the need to evaluate their impact with a focus on sex and gender differences.

In 2008, the longitudinal Dallas Lifespan Brain Study began collecting extensive data to explore factors influencing cognitive preservation and decline across the adult lifespan. This rich dataset, utilized in this project, includes participants' genetic and demographic information, along with amyloid accumulation data obtained from three waves of PET imaging.

This project aims to integrate this novel multiomics data with computational tools, to investigate sex differences among carriers of risk genes and their influence on amyloid PET imaging.

Dataset¶

The Dallas Lifespan Brain Study Data: https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds004856/versions/1.2.0

Sub-datasets used in this project:

  • "participants.tsv" - Contains demographic and cognitive measures for each patient (e.g., age, sex, BMI).
  • "Template19_Genotyping.xlsx" - Includes genotype data for each patient for four risk genes (APOE, BDNF, COMT, DRD2).
  • "Template17_Amyloid.xlsx", "Template17_Amyloid_wave2.xlsx", and "Template17_Amyloid_wave3.xlsx" - Contain amyloid measurements in different brain regions, with one dataframe per wave (three time points per patient).

Hypothesis¶

Throughout this project, we will be visualizing our data, to adress the following hypothesis:

  • Sex interacts with specific genetic variants (e.g., APOE, BDNF, COMT, DRD2) to influence Amyloid PET data.

To test this hypothesis, we will explore the following questions:

  1. What is the sex distribution among carriers of risk genes associated with Alzheimer's disease in the dataset?
  2. How do different genetic variants correlate to Amyloid data?
  3. How does amyloid distribution differ by sex across varying levels of genetic risk?

def join(df1, df2, how):
    """
    Joins two DataFrames based on the specified method.

    Parameters:
        df1 (DataFrame): The first DataFrame.
        df2 (DataFrame): The second DataFrame.
        how (str): The type of join to perform.
            - 'inner': Only rows with indices present in both DataFrames are kept.
            - 'outer': All indices from both DataFrames are kept, filling missing values with NaN.
            - 'left': Keep all indices from df1 and match rows from df2 where possible.
            - 'right': Keep all indices from df2 and match rows from df1 where possible.

    Returns:
        DataFrame: The resulting joined DataFrame.
    """
    return df1.join(df2, how=how)

Analysis (Visualization and Test)¶

To accurately compare the distribution of genetic variants between sexes, it is important to account for the difference in sample sizes (as there are more women than men). Using absolute values would lead to misleading conclusions. To address this, the calculate_percentage function was created. This function computes the relative distribution of variants within each sex, and the resulting percentages are used in the visualization instead of absolute values.

In [15]:
def calculate_percentage(df, x_variable, hue):
    """
    Calculates the relative distribution of the x_variable within each hue in a DataFrame.

    Parameters:
    - df (DataFrame): The DataFrame containing the data.
    - x_variable (str): The name of the column representing the variable to be analysed (e.g., "TotalE4Alleles").
    - hue (str): The name of the column representing the hue (e.g., "Sex").

    Returns:
    - prop_df: A DataFrame with the calculated proportions (prop).
    """
    prop_df = (df[x_variable]
               .groupby(df[hue])
               .value_counts(normalize=True)
               .rename("prop")
               .reset_index())
    
    return prop_df

This visualization does not show any significant differences between the sexes. To verify this hypothesis, a chi-square test is performed to assess the statistical significance of the observed differences. The chi-square test determines whether the distribution of categorical variables differs significantly from what would be expected by chance.

Test

In [17]:
def chi_test(df, gene, hue):
    """
    Perform a Chi-square test of independence between two categorical variables.

    Parameters:
    df (pandas.DataFrame): The DataFrame containing the data.
    gene (str): The name of the column representing the gene tested.
    hue (str): The name of the column representing the categorical variable (eg.Sex)

    Returns:
    dict: A dictionary containing the results of the chi-square test.
    """
    # Create a contingency table
    contingency_table = pd.crosstab(df[hue], df[gene])

    # Perform the chi-square test
    chi2_stat, p_value, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(contingency_table)

    # Return the test results in a dictionary
    return {"Gene": gene, "Chi2 Statistic": chi2_stat,
        "P-value": p_value, "Degrees of Freedom": dof
    }

def t_test(gene, risk):
    """
    Calculate a t-test the globalSUVR value in wave 1 and wave 3.
    """
    w1 = df_q2[(df_q2[gene] == risk) & (df_q2["Wave"] == 1)]["GlobalSUVR"]
    w3 = df_q2[(df_q2[gene] == risk) & (df_q2["Wave"] == 3)]["GlobalSUVR"]
    t,p = stats.ttest_rel(w1, w3)
    
    # Return the test results in a dictionary
    return {"Gene": gene, "T-value": t, "P-value": p}

Risk categories¶

The preceding comparison showed a relationship between the risk variants of the COMT and APOE genes and amyloid increase. In the next section, we will examine whether the number of risk variants across these genes in a patient also correlates with increased amyloid levels. This visualization will allow us to incorporate the 'sex' variable and determine whether there are differences between men and women.

To perform this comparison, we first assign values to the variants, following the same approach used for the APOE gene with the TotalE4Allele metric. The variant with the lowest risk for AD is assigned a value of 0, the moderate-risk variant is assigned 1, and the high-risk variant is assigned 2. Each patient is then categorized into one of three risk groups based on the sum of their risk values for the COMT and APOE genes. These categories, defined arbitrarily, are 'low' for a sum of 0 to 1, 'moderate' for a sum of 2, and 'high' for a sum of 3 to 4.

def define_risk(df, column_old, column_new, moderate, high):
    """
    Adds a new column to the given DataFrame, categorizing the values of an existing column into numeric risk levels.
    The new column is inserted immediately after the source column.

    Parameters:
    df : DataFrame
        The input DataFrame to which the new column will be added.
    column_old : str
        The name of the existing column in the DataFrame that contains the categorical risk levels to be mapped.
    column_new : str
        The name of the new column to be added to the DataFrame. This column will contain the numeric risk levels.
    moderate : str
        The category from `column_old` that represents a "moderate" level of risk.
    high : str
        The category from `column_old` that represents a "high" level of risk.
    Returns: None
    """
    position = df.columns.get_loc(column_old)+1
    risk_mapping = {high: 2, moderate: 1}
    new_column = df[column_old].map(risk_mapping).fillna(0).astype(int)
    df.insert(position, column_new, new_column)

Conclusion¶

  • What is the sex distribution among carriers of risk genes associated with Alzheimer's disease in the dataset?

The distribution of the risk alleles by sex is not significantly different in the observed dataset.

  • How do different genetic variants correlate to Amyloid data?

Significant correlations between the mean Global SUVR between wave 1 and wave 3 was shown for high risiko alleles of the COMT and APOE gene. This indicates that there is a correlation of these genetic variants with the amyloid distribution.

  • How does amyloid distribution differ by sex across varying levels of genetic risk?

The data revealed that high amyloid levels were predominantly observed in women. However, the number of participants in the high-amyloid group was insufficient to draw statistically significant conclusions.

Limitations¶

The dataset contained a higher proportion of female participants compared to males, which could introduce bias and lead to potential misinterpretations if the data is not normalized to account for the overall sample size.

Additionally, this project focused solely on global SUVR, despite evidence suggesting that amyloid distribution can vary across different brain regions.

Moreover, insufficient amyloid data was available for patients across all three waves, resulting in a limited sample size that may restrict the ability to draw statistically significant conclusions.
